\title{Jurassic Bitcoin: Ossified Tech Debt as an Isomorphic Mosquito for New Features}
\author{Patrick Dugan}
\date{April 17, 2026\\\small Revised October 8, 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Bitcoin's pre-SegWit consensus history contains behaviours that are no longer
desirable transaction forms but remain informative as design motifs. We isolate
three: \emph{transcript multiplicity}, \emph{identifier bifurcation}, and
\emph{carrier camouflage}. Each has a measurable legacy seam (FindAndDelete
context splits, \texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE} digest collapse, and the
\texttt{CHECKMULTISIG} dummy-element txid axis), each was deliberately closed at
the base layer by the BIP62, BIP66, BIP143, BIP147 and BIP341 lineage, and each
reappears as a \emph{controlled} degree of freedom in modern overlay protocols.
Our contribution is an observer pipeline that extracts historical windows,
materialises seam fixtures against a Bitcoin Core policy oracle, classifies the
resulting surfaces with a policy-envelope map, and lifts the abstractions into
live TradeLayer prototypes with BitVM-style dispute flows
\cite{linus2023bitvm,linus2025bitvm2,linus2026binohash,yang2021fluffy,kim2025fork}.
This revision adds a node-free analytic reproduction of the three seams (every
digest quoted here can be recomputed from one dependency-free script), a minimal
formalisation of what controlled freedom requires (semantic binding,
non-collapse, and authority separation), three Binohash-level primitives (one
per seam) that each carry a capability plain Script lacks and a deterministic
invariant, the UTXORef mechanism layer that turns the motifs into deterministic
handles bound to confirmed Bitcoin testnet4 evidence, and an explicit threat
model. The point is not to revive old bugs. It
is to translate fossilised Bitcoin behaviour into usable mechanics, in the
spirit of Robin Linus' Binohash.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

We use \emph{Jurassic Bitcoin} as shorthand for the historical period that
ends, in practical terms, before SegWit-era modularity takes over the protocol
surface. It names an older Bitcoin in which transaction behaviour still carried
many laissez-faire, lightly curated technical fingerprints of an earlier
engineering culture: behaviours that were tolerated, under-specified, or simply
left in place long enough to ossify. That period is over as a live design
regime, but its fossils remain informative. The claim of this paper is that some
of those neglected fingerprints are still \emph{isomorphically} usable as
protocol mechanics once they are abstracted away from their original,
historically contingent transaction forms.

If Jurassic Bitcoin supplies the fossils, the observer pipeline described here
is the mosquito in amber: it preserves small but information-rich traces of a
vanished protocol ecology, then lets us recover isomorphic mechanics without
reviving the original environment itself. SegWit's own witness space was later
used in ways nobody specified, through Ordinals and inscriptions
\cite{rodarmor2023ordinals}; whichever dinosaur one compares that to, the
footprint became part of Bitcoin history, and isomorphic utility of unintended
surfaces is real.

The central claim is that three motifs recur across both historical Bitcoin
and modern overlay design:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Transcript multiplicity}: one broad spend family can admit
multiple effective signing transcripts.
\item \textbf{Identifier bifurcation}: externally visible identifiers can move
while the committed core digest stays fixed.
\item \textbf{Carrier camouflage}: overlay commitments can hide inside ordinary
transaction topology rather than obvious exotic carriers.
\end{enumerate}

There is an inversion at the heart of the argument. At the base layer, every
one of these motifs was first encountered as a \emph{bug}: transaction
malleability, a degenerate signature digest, an unconstrained stack element.
The base layer responded by removing the freedom, culminating in the canonical
witness digests of BIP143 and BIP341
\cite{bip62,bip66,bip143,bip147,bip341,bip342}. Overlay protocols, which commit
to state through their own digests rather than through txids, can afford to
re-admit the same manifold shapes in a controlled way. Section~\ref{sec:formal}
states what ``controlled'' has to mean for that to be safe.

We study the motifs with a deterministic observability toolchain and then lift
them into live prototypes where BitVM-style routes, challenges and resolutions
can be exercised, without claiming that the legacy Bitcoin seams themselves are
production-ready constructions. The perspective sits downstream of BitVM's
dispute-oriented execution model and of Binohash-style transaction
introspection, while remaining methodologically closer to differential
blockchain testing than to new consensus design
\cite{linus2023bitvm,linus2025bitvm2,linus2026binohash,yang2021fluffy,kim2025fork}.

\paragraph{What changed in this revision.} The April draft reported the three
motifs and the Litecoin grafts. Since then the Bitcoin-side status moved: a
TradeLayer grant transaction and a Taproot reserve vault were confirmed on
Bitcoin testnet4 and bound into an off-chain route referee that consumes quirk
route candidates (Section~\ref{sec:handles}). This revision also adds the
analytic reproduction of the seams (Section~\ref{sec:measured}), the
formalisation (Section~\ref{sec:formal}), a related-work survey of the BIP
lineage and of the newer introspection tricks that build on the same seams
(Section~\ref{sec:related}), a threat model (Section~\ref{sec:threats}), and
corrections of record, including the fact that the repository's ``2013 payout
burst'' window at heights 279150--279320 was mined in January 2014.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A reproducible \emph{historical-to-modern} workflow for turning Bitcoin
fossils into measured experimental surfaces, with stable specimen identifiers
and paper-ready report generation.
\item Three overlay-relevant motifs, each anchored to a legacy seam that the
harness measures at the digest level, together with a dependency-free analytic
reproduction so the cryptographic facts can be checked without a node.
\item A minimal formalisation of controlled freedom: four properties
(semantic binding, non-collapse, authority separation, rotation invariance)
that separate a usable isomorphism from a re-imported malleability bug.
\item Three Binohash-level primitives, one per seam (append-only aggregatable
attestation, state-bound handle rotation, and a Binohash parameter bench),
each demonstrated by a deterministic node-free invariant and annotated with the
admission properties it enforces.
\item A policy-envelope mapping method that separates replay-only fossils,
ordinary historical cover, and clean modern isomorphisms.
\item Live prototype grafts: nine LTCTEST procedural-token flow graphs, eight of
them with dated live-run artifacts, with BitVM-style dispute edges; the UTXORef
mechanism-handle layer; and a two-stage referee bound to confirmed Bitcoin
testnet4 transactions.
\item An explicit threat model, including the consequence of Bitcoin Core 30.0's
datacarrier policy change for the carrier-camouflage motif.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Legacy signature hashing and its seams}

Before BIP143, every signature in Bitcoin committed to a digest produced by one
function, \texttt{SignatureHash(scriptCode, tx, nIn, nHashType)}: blank every
\texttt{scriptSig}, substitute \texttt{scriptCode} at input \texttt{nIn}, apply
the \texttt{SIGHASH} mode, serialise, append the hash type, and double-SHA256.
Three details of that function are the seams this paper measures
\cite{bitcoincore30src}.

\paragraph{FindAndDelete.} In legacy \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIG} and
\texttt{OP\_CHECKMULTISIG}, every signature being checked is first deleted from
\texttt{scriptCode} wherever its push occurs at an opcode boundary, originally
so that a signature cannot be asked to sign itself. The consequence is that the
digest depends on the \emph{multiset of signature pushes that happen to appear
in the script}. For ordinary scripts this is a no-op; for a script that
contains the signature bytes it is a transcript-selection knob.

\paragraph{\texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE} degeneracy.} If the signed input index has
no corresponding output, \texttt{SignatureHash} returns the constant
\texttt{uint256::ONE} instead of a digest. A signature over that constant
verifies for any transaction that reaches the same branch with the same key.
BIP143 removed the behaviour for witness programs by substituting an all-zero
\texttt{hashOutputs} in that case \cite{bip143}.

\paragraph{The \texttt{CHECKMULTISIG} dummy element.} Legacy
\texttt{OP\_CHECKMULTISIG} pops one extra, unused stack element. Its content
does not enter the digest but does enter the serialised transaction, so it moves
the txid without invalidating any signature. BIP62 catalogued it as a
malleability source, \texttt{NULLDUMMY} became relay policy, and BIP147 made it
consensus for witness version 0 \cite{bip62,bip147}.

\subsection{How the base layer closed the seams}

The lineage is worth stating because it is the reason these surfaces are
fossils rather than live affordances. BIP62 enumerated third-party malleability
and was withdrawn in favour of a structural fix; BIP66 made DER encoding strict;
BIP141 and BIP143 moved witnesses out of the txid and introduced a digest with
no FindAndDelete and no constant-one branch; BIP147 enforced the null dummy;
BIP341 and BIP342 dropped \texttt{OP\_CHECKMULTISIG} from Tapscript entirely in
favour of \texttt{OP\_CHECKSIGADD} and committed the position of
\texttt{OP\_CODESEPARATOR} in the digest
\cite{bip62,bip66,bip141,bip143,bip147,bip341,bip342}. Legacy outputs remain
spendable, so consensus still evaluates the seams today; policy fences them. That
split between consensus-live and policy-dead is exactly what the harness is
built to measure (Section~\ref{sec:toolchain}).

\subsection{Introspection and dispute systems built on the same seams}

BitVM reframed Bitcoin Script as a verifier of optimistic computation claims:
commit, challenge, and expose one incorrect step \cite{linus2023bitvm}. BitVM2
turned that into a permissionless bridge design with a light-client-based
pegout \cite{linus2025bitvm2}. Binohash is the result most relevant here: it
exploits FindAndDelete in legacy \texttt{OP\_CHECKMULTISIG} together with
proof-of-work signature grinding to obtain a collision-resistant digest of the
spending transaction that is readable in Script, giving limited transaction
introspection without a soft fork \cite{linus2026binohash}. Binohash is a
demonstration that the FindAndDelete seam is not merely a museum piece; this
paper's harness is the bench on which such constructions can be measured
against the actual policy envelope. Heilman's Lamport-signature construction,
which reads ECDSA signature length through \texttt{OP\_SIZE}, is a second
example of a legacy encoding seam re-used as a feature \cite{heilman2024lamport},
as are Poelstra's Schnorr-plus-\texttt{OP\_CAT} tricks \cite{poelstra2021cat}.
Covenant proposals such as \texttt{CHECKTEMPLATEVERIFY}, \texttt{ANYPREVOUT},
and \texttt{OP\_CAT} are the soft-fork path to the same expressiveness
\cite{bip119,bip118,bip347,moser2016covenants}; the motifs in this paper are what
an overlay can do while those proposals remain unactivated.

\subsection{Overlay systems whose coordination surfaces we target}

The modern isomorphisms are drawn from systems whose public coordination
surfaces are richer than their on-chain footprint: Lightning channels and
watchtowers \cite{poon2016lightning,mccorry2019pisa}, discreet log contracts and
their oracle attestations \cite{dryja2017dlc}, Ark rounds and VTXOs
\cite{keceli2023ark}, statechain handoffs \cite{somsen2018statechains}, and
Taproot Assets proof anchors and universes \cite{osuntokun2022taprootassets}.
Gudgeon et al.\ give the systematisation of layer-two protocols that these
examples sit inside \cite{gudgeon2020sok}. In each case there is a committed
core (a channel state, an outcome, a VTXO, a handoff, an asset transition) and
several public handles, transcripts, or publication shapes around it.

\subsection{Differential and historical testing}

The observer pipeline is methodologically closest to consensus-differential
systems that use executable artifacts and cross-checking harnesses as research
instruments: Fluffy's multi-transaction differential fuzzing of Ethereum
clients and fork-state-aware differential fuzzing of consensus implementations
\cite{yang2021fluffy,kim2025fork}. The difference in emphasis is that we are
not primarily hunting divergences between two implementations; we are using a
Core oracle plus a narrow shadow executor to \emph{stratify} known seams by
era, digest behaviour, and policy outcome.

\subsection{Publication policy drift}
\label{sec:drift}

Carrier camouflage was framed in April against a world where explicit data
carriers were policy-limited. Bitcoin Core 30.0 raised the default
\texttt{-datacarriersize} to 100{,}000 bytes and admits multiple
\texttt{OP\_RETURN} outputs per transaction for relay and mining
\cite{bitcoincore30}. Explicit carriers are therefore no longer gated by
default policy. The consequence for Motif~III is that camouflage no longer buys
\emph{admission}; it buys resistance to fingerprinting and a cost profile that
matches ordinary traffic. Section~\ref{sec:threats} returns to this.

\texttt{fetch-fixtures}, \texttt{mint-*-seam}, \texttt{summarize},
\texttt{museum}, \texttt{report}) over four layers
(Figure~\ref{fig:observer-pipeline}).

Mainnet windows are selected around activation boundaries and structurally
interesting transaction families, then extracted into reproducible corpora
(Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The pre-SegWit tour is intended to end at CSV rather

\paragraph{Seam-family fixtures and replay.}
Curated fixture families capture legacy behaviours such as FindAndDelete
context splits, \texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE} degeneracy, and \texttt{CHECKMULTISIG}
dummy-element txid variation. Fixtures are minted on regtest with real funding
outpoints, replayed through \texttt{testmempoolaccept}, executed by the Rust
shadow, and reported with stable identifiers: a \texttt{specimen\_id} is the
SHA-256 of the canonical (key-sorted) JSON of the reduced testcase, so two
operators who mint the same seam obtain the same identifier. Each specimen
carries the derived legacy sighash digest, txid, policy outcome and reason, and
quirk-family fields such as removal counts or dummy-element size.

\paragraph{Historical carrier and sidecar benches.}
The toolchain also supports ordinary-looking carrier families in the
279150--279320 window, especially payout-shaped topologies, so that overlay
publication ideas can be discussed in terms of historical cover rather than only
abstract commitment formats (Section~\ref{sec:carriers}).

\paragraph{Modern live graft harnesses.}
TradeLayer on Litecoin testnet (LTCTEST) is used as a controllable live
environment for procedural-token flows, oracle relays, BitVM-style caches,
challenges, resolutions and payout edges, and Bitcoin testnet4 is used for the
reserve-bound referee layer (Section~\ref{sec:handles}). This gives a live
isomorphism layer without claiming that the exact historical Bitcoin transaction
forms survive unchanged.

\paragraph{Oracle scope.} The Core oracle is \texttt{testmempoolaccept} on a
current node. Epoch flags from the consensus profile are an organisational lens
for stratified replay, not proof of full historical block-validation semantics
at height $H$. The Rust shadow is intentionally narrow (one input, a P2WPKH-style
opcode slice, deterministic signature hooks); it exists to classify, not to
validate.

\section{Three Design Motifs}
\label{sec:motifs}

\subsection{Motif I: Transcript Multiplicity}

One broad spend family can admit multiple effective signing transcripts. In the
legacy seam bench, FindAndDelete subset variation and \texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE}
degeneracy both show that small structural changes can alter the effective
digest, or collapse it, without changing the broader transaction family. The
important abstraction is not the historical script quirk; it is the existence
of a transcript-selection manifold with two distinct regions: a benign region in
which several transcripts alias to one digest class, and a hazardous region in
which the digest collapses to a verifier-known constant. Binohash lives in the
first region and makes it load-bearing \cite{linus2026binohash}; the
constant-one branch lives in the second and must be fenced.

Modern overlays want this because BitVM- and DLC-adjacent systems depend on
alternative off-chain transcripts, branch packages, reveal paths and dispute
narratives. A designer may want the same higher-level state machine while
preserving freedom over transcript selection, packaging, or attestation form:
a compact relay form and a full evidence form of one proof, or a success
package and a timeout package over one claim.

\subsection{Motif II: Identifier Bifurcation}

A public identifier can vary while the committed core stays fixed. The
dummy-element seam is the canonical example: txids move while the legacy
sighash digest is invariant. The legacy path is policy-rejected today, but it
exposes a clean separation between \emph{semantic commitment} and
\emph{public handle}. Classical Bitcoin work encountered this family as a
malleability problem rather than as a design affordance, which makes the
historical contrast useful \cite{andrychowicz2013malleability,decker2014malleability}.

Practical systems need visible session identifiers, anchor labels, namespace
handles, rendezvous tags and publication references even when the semantic core
is fixed elsewhere. The motif says: derive every such handle from the core
commitment plus a role and a label, so that rotation is free and verification is
unchanged.

\subsection{Motif III: Carrier Camouflage}

Overlay commitments should be able to hide inside ordinary-looking transaction
topology rather than obviously exotic publication surfaces. The historical
carrier bench shows that strong candidate covers already existed in ordinary
payout and redistribution topologies: high-fanout sprays, exact-value batch
redistributions, and large aggregators (Table~\ref{tab:carriers}). The point is
not simply that big transactions existed. The point is that ordinary historical
cover is a model for how modern overlays should think about publication shape,
ambient noise and carrier selection. This is adjacent in spirit to
transaction-shape privacy work such as PayJoin, where the operational question
is how to inhabit ordinary-looking transaction space without leaking a
distinctive surface \cite{bip78,ghesmati2022payjoin}.

\section{Measured Seam Surfaces}
\label{sec:measured}

\subsection{Regtest-funded seams}

Three seam manifests are minted with real regtest prevouts so that Core returns
policy-specific reasons instead of \texttt{missing-inputs}. The
\texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE} manifest has four specimens (two bug forms at a
pre-BIP16 representative height, two controls at a post-BIP34 height, each with
and without \texttt{ANYONECANPAY}); the DUMMYGRIND manifest has three (empty,
one-byte zero, and 32-byte dummies); the FindAndDelete manifest has three
signature subsets at the BIP16 boundary height. In the funded runs the
\texttt{aa} and \texttt{aaaa} subsets share one digest while \texttt{aabb}
produces a distinct digest under the same policy rejection reason; the two
\texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE} bug specimens collapse to the constant-one digest while
the controls diverge; and the non-empty dummy variants change the txid while
preserving the sighash digest and are rejected under a \texttt{NULLDUMMY}-style
reason. The companion note \emph{Jurassic Surfaces} renders those runs as
\texttt{report --format latex} tables directly from the artifact directories.

\subsection{Analytic reproduction without a node}

The cryptographic content of each seam is a pure function of legacy
serialisation and the original \texttt{SignatureHash}, so it can be reproduced
without a node. \texttt{scripts/seam\_digests\_standalone.py} ports
\texttt{FindAndDelete} and \texttt{SignatureHash} verbatim (including the
\texttt{uint256::ONE} branches), builds deterministic specimens from
SHA-256-derived placeholder keys and prevouts, and emits the tables below. No
signature is verified; the claim is about digest classes and txids, which do
not depend on key validity. Digests are rendered byte-reversed, as
\texttt{uint256::GetHex()} does, so the constant-one digest prints as
$0^{63}1$, matching the tripwire value used in Section~\ref{sec:handles}.

\paragraph{Seam A: FindAndDelete context split.} The redeemScript is
\texttt{<aa> OP\_DROP <bb> OP\_DROP OP\_2 <pkA> <pkB> <pkC> OP\_3 OP\_CHECKMULTISIG}:
a script that contains the one-byte ``signature'' pushes it will be fed. That
is the only kind of script for which legacy FindAndDelete changes the
scriptCode, and it is a crafted, legacy-only degree of freedom, which is the
point. Table~\ref{tab:seamA} shows four signature multisets. Removing
\texttt{aa} once or twice yields the same scriptCode (deletion is idempotent
per push class), so \texttt{subset\_aa} and \texttt{subset\_aaaa} alias to one
digest; \texttt{subset\_aabb} deletes a second push class and splits; the
baseline with a signature absent from the script is a third class. Every row has
a distinct txid because the \texttt{scriptSig} differs; the digest classes are
what the motif is about.

\paragraph{Seam C: DUMMYGRIND.} A 1-of-1 P2SH multisig spend varies only the
dummy element: the empty vector (\texttt{00}), a one-byte zero push
(\texttt{01 00}), and a 32-byte zero push. The legacy digest blanks every
\texttt{scriptSig} and substitutes the redeemScript, so all three share one
digest while the serialised transaction, and therefore the txid, differs
(Table~\ref{tab:seamC}). Note that \texttt{CScript} encodes a one-byte vector as
\texttt{01 xx} and never as \texttt{OP\_1..OP\_16}, which is why the empty and
one-byte-zero dummies are distinct bytes with the same numeric value. The
32-byte variant shows the shape of the affordance: a 256-bit field in the
unlocking data that moves the public identifier and nothing else. Relay policy
rejects every non-empty dummy under \texttt{NULLDUMMY}; witness version 0
rejects it by consensus \cite{bip147}.

\section{Controlled Freedom: A Minimal Formalisation}
\label{sec:formal}

The motifs are safe only when the freedom they introduce sits above a fixed
commitment rather than beside it. The following is deliberately minimal; it is
what the prototypes actually enforce, not a security proof.

Let $S$ be an overlay semantic state (a lease, an oracle outcome, a VTXO
transition, a reserve route) and let $c = H(S)$ be its canonical commitment
under a collision-resistant hash of a canonical serialisation. Define:

\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathcal{T}(c)$, the set of \emph{transcripts} a verifier accepts as
evidence for $c$ (compact and full proof packages, retry-equivalent success
proofs, branch-specific challenge packages). Transcript multiplicity is the
design decision $|\mathcal{T}(c)| > 1$.
\item $\mathcal{N}(c)$, the set of \emph{public handles} that resolve to $c$
(rendezvous labels, proof anchors, round ids, alert handles), with a resolution
map $\nu : \mathcal{N}(c) \to \{c\}$. Identifier bifurcation is the design
decision that $\nu$ is many-to-one and that rotating within $\mathcal{N}(c)$ is
a no-op on $S$.
\item $\mathcal{C}$, a family of \emph{carrier} transaction shapes with a shape
statistic $\sigma$ (input and output counts, output-type histogram, repeated
amounts, largest-output share). Carrier camouflage is the design decision to
publish under a carrier whose $\sigma$ is drawn from an ambient distribution of
ordinary traffic.
\end{itemize}

Four properties separate a usable isomorphism from a re-imported bug:

\begin{description}
\item[P1, semantic binding.] Every $t \in \mathcal{T}(c)$ commits to $c$: if the
verifier accepts $t$ for $c'$ then $c' = c$. Aliases are aliases \emph{of a
commitment}, never free-floating evidence.
\item[P2, non-collapse.] No transcript digest in $\mathcal{T}(c)$, and no
candidate the verifier is willing to promote to an identifier, equals a
verifier-known constant. Concretely, $0^{63}1$ is rejected before it can become
a funding, claim or challenge id. The constant-one branch is the historical
instance; the property generalises to any degenerate digest.
\item[P3, authority separation.] Admitting a transcript, handle or carrier never
substitutes for spend authority. A route transcript candidate is not a spend
authorisation; admissibility additionally requires live reserve evidence and an
exact payout-vector commitment (Section~\ref{sec:handles}).
\item[P4, rotation invariance.] For all $n, n' \in \mathcal{N}(c)$ the
verifier's decision is identical. Handles index; they do not authorise.
\end{description}

The contrast with base-layer malleability is now precise. Pre-BIP62
malleability was multiplicity over the \emph{txid}, in a system where the txid
was also the commitment that downstream transactions spent from, so there was
no stable $c$ above the moving identifier. The base-layer fix made the digest
and the txid canonical. Overlays can re-admit multiplicity because their $c$ is
not the txid: the seams have the same manifold shape, bound at a different
point. That is what ``isomorphic'' means in the title.

\subsection{Quirk-indexed route referee on Bitcoin testnet4}

The route referee binds a quirk route candidate to live reserve evidence before
a BitVM route or spend proposal is treated as admissible application state. Its
rule is the operational form of P3:

\begin{center}
\texttt{route transcript candidate $\neq$ spend authority.}
\end{center}

A claim core carries the motif, a public handle, \texttt{semanticStateHash},
\texttt{routeTranscriptHash}, \texttt{withdrawalRootHex},
\texttt{finalOutputVectorHash}, \texttt{liveTraceHash}, the

vault, the withdrawal root matches the UTXORef withdrawal queue, the final output

CSV-safe. Four demo outcomes exercise the motifs: a compact transcript alias is
accepted, a namespace-rotated handle over the same grant state is accepted, a
mutated withdrawal root is rejected with reason \texttt{withdrawal\_root}, and
an unknown route transcript is rejected with reason
\texttt{route\_transcript\_candidate}.

\section{Binohash-Level Primitives}
\label{sec:primitives}

The mechanism handles of Section~\ref{sec:handles} package the motifs for
existing prototypes. This section goes one level deeper and asks the Binohash
question directly \cite{linus2026binohash}: can each seam carry a
\emph{named primitive} with a capability that plain Script lacks, a precise
precondition, and an honest cost or policy line? We give three, each
demonstrated by a deterministic, node-free invariant in
\texttt{scripts/binohash\_level\_tools.py}. As with the seam reproduction, no
signature is verified; the demonstration is a digest-level identity or
collision bound, which does not depend on key validity. Every invariant below
is reported \texttt{PASS} by the script, and each primitive is annotated with
the admission properties (P1--P4) of Section~\ref{sec:formal} that it enforces.

\subsection{Primitive 1: Append-Only Aggregatable Attestation}

The constructive half of the \texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE} seam is not the collapse
but \texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE|ANYONECANPAY}. Under that flag a signer's digest is
a function of only its own input, its own output, and its index: ANYONECANPAY
drops every other input from the hash, and SINGLE nulls the earlier outputs and
truncates away the later ones. Independently produced ``slots'' therefore
compose into one transaction without a re-signing round. This is the mechanism
behind assurance contracts \cite{bitcoinwiki_contracts}; we lift it to
threshold oracle attestation, where each oracle signs one
(funding slot, payout slot) pair binding a shared event hash and an aggregator
assembles any sufficient subset.

The script demonstrates four facts about a three-oracle example. First, the
checked signer's digest is unchanged when every other slot's input and output
content is mutated (\texttt{digest\_independent\_of\_other\_slot\_content}):
content independence is what lets slots be produced in isolation. Second,
appending a later oracle slot leaves all earlier signers' digests fixed
(\texttt{append\_later\_slot\_preserves\_earlier\_digests}): aggregation is
append-only. Third, removing an \emph{earlier} slot changes a signer's digest
(\texttt{remove\_earlier\_slot\_changes\_digest}), because SINGLE binds the
signer to its output index; an aggregator may append and may rewrite other
slots' contents, but it may not drop or reorder earlier slots. Fourth, a slot
whose signed index has no output collapses to the constant one and is refused
(\texttt{constant\_one\_collapse\_detected\_and\_refused}), which is property P2
enforced at the point of use. The primitive enforces P1 (each payout output
carries the event hash), P2, and P3 (a slot is evidence of one attestation, not
spend authority over the aggregate). The precondition, stated honestly, is that
signers agree on \texttt{nVersion} and \texttt{nLockTime} and that composition
preserves index order.

\subsection{Primitive 2: State-Bound Handle Rotation}

The dummy-element seam moves the txid while the sighash is fixed, but the raw
seam gives no way for a third party to tell an authentic rotation from an
unrelated malleation. The primitive supplies the missing derivation:
\[
\texttt{handle} = H(\texttt{stateHash} \,\|\, \texttt{label} \,\|\, \texttt{epoch}),
\]
so an indexer verifies a rotation by one recomputation (property P4) and the
handle is bound to the committed state (property P1) yet is never read by any
spend path (property P3). The script shows two carriers for the same rotation.
The \emph{legacy} carrier places the handle in the unused
\texttt{CHECKMULTISIG} dummy: three epochs give three distinct txids over one
fixed sighash digest (\texttt{legacy\_txid\_moves\_digest\_fixed}), the historic
mechanism, which is policy-dead under \texttt{NULLDUMMY} and consensus-invalid
for witness v0 \cite{bip147}. The \emph{witness-era} carrier is the deployable
one: the committed state is anchored once in a byte-identical
\texttt{OP\_RETURN} output while the rotating handle rides a second
\texttt{OP\_RETURN} output, so the anchor is stable across epochs
(\texttt{witness\_anchor\_fixed\_across\_rotation}) and the whole shape is
relay-standard under Bitcoin Core 30.0 \cite{bitcoincore30}. All six handles
recompute from the state (\texttt{all\_handles\_verify}). The overlay use is a
rotating rendezvous, watchtower-alert, or proof-anchor handle over a fixed
channel or reserve state, without re-anchoring the state. The honesty line from
Section~\ref{sec:threats} carries over: rotation defeats third-party indexing,
not linkage by anyone who holds \texttt{stateHash}.

\subsection{Primitive 3: A Binohash Parameter Bench}

The third primitive does not propose a competing scheme; it is a cost
accountant for Binohash itself, so a protocol designer can choose parameters
against a real line rather than a feeling \cite{linus2026binohash}. The script
first reproduces the concrete degree of freedom: a redeemScript that embeds the
pushes it is fed, so after FindAndDelete the signed scriptCode is a function of
chosen data (\texttt{scriptcode\_is\_data\_dependent}). That is the surface
Binohash grinds against. It then tabulates Linus' two-round parameters: with
weights $W_1, W_2$ the digest has $W_1 + W_2$ bits of collision resistance at

weights are already economically out of reach. The value of the bench is that
the knee of that curve is where a BitVM-bridge designer must sit: enough
resistance to bind an introspected transaction, little enough work to grind at

\subsection{What ``live'' means here}

These are demonstrations, not broadcasts. Primitive 1's composability and
Primitive 2's rotation are exact digest identities that hold for any key, and
Primitive 3's bound is Linus' analysis reproduced against a cost model; all
three run offline and deterministically. The nearest deployed relatives are
the assurance-contract pattern for Primitive 1
\cite{bitcoinwiki_contracts}, the oracle-sidecar and watchtower-beacon LTCTEST
meshes already in the repository for Primitives 1 and 2, and BitVM bridges for
Primitive 3 \cite{linus2025bitvm2}. We deliberately stop short of broadcasting
funded Bitcoin transactions for these primitives: that step is the funded
oracle-sidecar materialisation listed as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:threats}, and it is gated on the same prevout funding as the
replay bench.

\section{BitVM Prototype Applications}
\label{sec:bitvm}

The live prototype layer is deliberately framed as \emph{BitVM-style}
application work, not as a claim that the legacy quirks implement BitVM
\cite{linus2023bitvm}.

\subsection{Motif wrapper versus vanilla BitVM}

Vanilla BitVM is a dispute-oriented execution pattern: participants commit to a
computation or state transition, and if one party publishes an incorrect claim,
the protocol exposes it through a challenge path. The Jurassic grafts do not
change that security model and do not require changing Bitcoin Core. They add a
deterministic application wrapper with three jobs. Transcript multiplicity lets
one committed state carry compact and full transcript packages, so a dispute
graph can choose between short relay forms, full evidence forms, or
challenge-specific witness narratives. Identifier bifurcation separates public
handles from the semantic core, so an oracle event, a Taproot Assets proof
anchor, a watchtower alert, a router branch, or a proof package can all point
back to one commitment without overloading one identifier. Carrier camouflage
treats publication as a placement problem and attaches carrier hints such as
payout batches, rebalances, sweeps, wallet batches, or distribution shadows.

The resulting architecture is not ``better BitVM consensus.'' It is a
product-facing packaging layer. A vanilla flow provides the cache, challenge,
resolution and payout edges; the wrapper adds transcript aliases, namespace
handles, carrier hints, procedural-token state and observer artifacts that make
those edges easier to route, index, audit and integrate. The cost is that the
application layer acquires explicit watcher, indexing, naming and
artifact-management requirements. Those are acceptable in the prototypes because
they are deterministic and visible in the flow graph; they are not a
simplification of BitVM's dispute assumptions.

\subsection{Procedural-token flow graphs}

procedural-token flow graphs. The fused LTCTEST experiment

\texttt{aa} and \texttt{aaaa}, two namespace attestations whose blob references

excess \texttt{bitvm\_cache} branches, and sends one through

\subsection{Transcript selector, anchor search, carrier-style routing}

Transcript multiplicity suggests a search bench for alternative dispute
transcripts, branch packaging and attestation forms; Binohash is the relevant
comparison point because it makes transaction introspection itself a
programmable input to BitVM-adjacent designs \cite{linus2026binohash}.
Identifier bifurcation suggests search surfaces for public handles: session
ids, anchor names, rendezvous labels, publication namespaces. Carrier
camouflage suggests that publication and settlement need not be isolated
\texttt{OP\_RETURN}-style acts but can be embedded in ordinary-looking payout
and redistribution shapes, with one route proceeding to release while another is
challenged and blocked.

\section{Applications Beyond BitVM}
\label{sec:beyond}

The same three motifs admit non-BitVM application meshes, and the repository
includes live LTCTEST proofs for several.

\paragraph{Oracle sidecars.} The \emph{oracle sidecar mesh} binds one semantic
sidecar state to two transcript aliases (``fast'' and ``full'' attestations),
two public namespace handles, and two payout-shaped carrier hints modelled on
the spray and exact-value specimens of Table~\ref{tab:carriers}. The Bitcoin
replay bench for the same sidecars is where P2 first appeared operationally: the
synthetic casebook includes two constant-one publications that the hazard
filter rejects by construction.

\paragraph{Watchtower beacons.} The \emph{watchtower beacon mesh} expresses one
alert state through compact and full proof transcripts with a rotating public
alert handle and rebalance- or sweep-shaped carrier hints. The testnet4 cadence
referee is the reserve-bound continuation of this mesh. Pisa's
accountable-watchtower model is the natural comparison: our contribution is the
packaging and publication surface, not the accountability mechanism
\cite{mccorry2019pisa}.

\paragraph{Statechain handoffs.} The \emph{statechain handoff mesh} treats an
ownership transition as a fixed core with acknowledgement and finalisation
transcripts, rotated checkpoint handles, and ordinary checkpoint publication
shapes \cite{somsen2018statechains}.

\paragraph{Taproot Assets anchors.} The \emph{Taproot Assets anchor mesh}
splits one asset transition into compact and full proof packages, rotates
asset-id and universe-anchor handles, and publishes under wallet-batch or
distribution-shadow hints \cite{osuntokun2022taprootassets}; the UTXORef RFQ
prototype carries the corresponding handle ids.

\paragraph{Lightning deposits into tokenised BTC.} The LN-to-\texttt{tlBTC}
path that produced the testnet4 grant is itself a small instance of all three
motifs: the deposit carries a wallet invoice receipt, a bridge receipt and a
grant transcript that all bind one payment hash; the invoice id, request id and
property id are distinct handles over that hash; and the on-chain action is an
ordinary managed-token grant rather than a bespoke transaction shape (with the
qualification of Section~\ref{sec:handles} that the grant is an explicit
\texttt{OP\_RETURN}).

\section{Threat Model and Limitations}
\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Re-imported malleability.} If an overlay keys state on a transcript
digest rather than on $c$, aliases become double-count or replay vectors: a
retry-equivalent success proof would be credited twice. P1 and application-level
idempotence on $\mathcal{T}(c)$ are the mitigations, and the prototypes mark
retry-equivalent packages explicitly so that settlement logic can collapse them.

\paragraph{Collapse hazards beyond the constant one.} P2 names $0^{63}1$ because
that is the historical instance, but any digest that is independent of the
claimed state is equally dangerous. The tripwire should be a predicate over
degenerate inputs (out-of-range indices, empty output sets, zero-length
transcripts), not a single blacklisted value.

\paragraph{Handle rotation is not privacy against the verifier.} Rotation
defeats naive indexing by third parties, but anyone holding $h$ can link every
handle in $\mathcal{N}(c)$, by construction. The motif is a namespace property;
it is not an unlinkability guarantee. Rotation also raises observer cost: a
watcher must index every handle class, which is why the prototypes emit the
handle derivations as artifacts.

\paragraph{Carrier camouflage: efficacy and ethics.} With Core 30.0 policy
(Section~\ref{sec:drift}), explicit carriers are relay-standard, so camouflage
no longer buys admission. What remains is resistance to protocol
fingerprinting and a fee profile aligned with ordinary traffic. That is a
legitimate goal with precedent in PayJoin \cite{bip78}, but ``ordinary-looking''
can also be read as evasion of a node operator's stated policy. We restrict the
claim to protocol self-fingerprinting, and we note that the Bitcoin-side
artifacts produced so far are explicit \texttt{OP\_RETURN} transactions: the
camouflage thesis is demonstrated at the referee layer and on LTCTEST, not yet
in Bitcoin's on-chain footprint.

\paragraph{Oracle and shadow limits.} \texttt{testmempoolaccept} reports current
policy at the tip, not historical block validation; the Rust shadow executes a
narrow opcode slice; the synthetic Bitcoin replay fixtures stop at
\texttt{missing-inputs} until real prevouts are funded. The analytic
reproduction of Section~\ref{sec:measured} is deliberately independent of all
three so that the digest-level claims do not inherit those limits.

\paragraph{Litecoin is not Bitcoin.} LTCTEST is a live engineering environment
with TradeLayer available, not evidence about Bitcoin consensus. The testnet4
work narrows that gap for the reserve-bound referees but not for the
procedural-token flows.

\paragraph{Snapshot evidence.} The reserve outpoint bound by the July referee
runs has since been spent (Table~\ref{tab:anchors}). Admission is relative to a
live import, and the referee rejects stale trace hashes by design; readers
reproducing the demo should expect a fresh import to change the admitted set.

\paragraph{No on-chain enforcement.} The mechanism handles and both referees are
off-chain objects. They decide what an application treats as admissible
evidence and produce challenge evidence for disputes; they do not change the
Taproot vault spend path, Bitcoin consensus, or BitVM's underlying dispute
assumptions.

\section{Discussion}

The useful interpretation of these results is not that old Bitcoin bugs should
be revived. It is that historical Bitcoin reveals protocol degrees of freedom
that can be abstracted and measured. Some are cryptographic, such as digest
aliasing or txid movement; others are topological, such as publication inside
ordinary payout structure. The project's value lies in making those degrees of
freedom reproducible, classifiable and portable into modern experimental
grafts, with the four properties of Section~\ref{sec:formal} as the admission
test. That Binohash independently made the FindAndDelete seam load-bearing for
transaction introspection suggests the vein is real; this paper's claim is
narrower and complementary, namely that the same seams, read as motifs, give
overlay designers a vocabulary for coordination surfaces they already build by
hand.

\section{Future Work}

\paragraph{Bitcoin-side materialisation.} Since April, the testnet4 grant and
reserve vault have been confirmed and bound into the referees. The remaining
Bitcoin-side blocker is funded-prevout materialisation for the oracle-sidecar
replay bench, so that the carrier-camouflage thesis can be evaluated against a
live Bitcoin carrier rather than an explicit \texttt{OP\_RETURN}. A second step
is to re-import the reserve after its spend and record how admission changes.

\paragraph{Reusable overlay templates.} The UTXORef mechanism catalogue is a
first step from scenario-specific harnesses toward parametric declarations of
relay preludes, routing buckets and dispute edges. The next step is to make the
flow-graph fixtures the source of truth for both the LTCTEST runners and the
referees.

\paragraph{Generalised tripwire.} Replace the single constant-one check with a
predicate over degenerate digest inputs, and add the \texttt{SIGHASH\_SINGLE}
hazard family to the regtest seams as policy-stamped controls.

\paragraph{Mainnet Litecoin and later Bitcoin windows.} Rerun the ossification
walk against Litecoin mainnet, and extend the Bitcoin tour through the BIP65
and CSV boundaries listed in Table~\ref{tab:windows}.

\paragraph{Binohash-style adaptation by other developers.} The broader agenda
is to encourage other Bitcoin developers to mine these motifs: alternative
transcript alias surfaces for challenge systems, new namespace or anchor search
constructions, ordinary transaction topology as commitment cover, contract-layer
analogues of the same manifold shapes, and DLC or BitVM systems that use relay
and identifier freedom without depending on policy-rejected legacy forms
\cite{linus2026binohash}. The message to the community is intentionally
modest: take these motifs, test them inside your own covenant, DLC, watchtower,
wallet-coordination or BitVM-adjacent setting, and see whether they open design
space without inheriting the old pathologies.

\section*{Conclusion}

Bitcoin's old quirks are not just anecdotes. When measured carefully, they
define reproducible search surfaces. Transcript multiplicity, identifier
bifurcation and carrier camouflage together form a compact vocabulary for
reasoning about how ossified historical behaviour can inspire modern overlay
systems, and semantic binding, non-collapse, authority separation and rotation
invariance are the conditions under which that inspiration is safe to act on.
The toolchain, the analytic reproduction, the three Binohash-level primitives,
the Litecoin grafts and the testnet4-bound referees in these repositories show
that the motifs can be extracted, classified, formalised, turned into specified
primitives with checkable invariants, and lifted into live BitVM-style and
non-BitVM applications without confusing historical fossils for deployable
mechanisms.
